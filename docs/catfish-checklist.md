# How to spot a catfish: a checklist

A catfish is someone using another person's photos to pretend to be somebody they are not. Most are caught by the same handful of signs. None of these is proof on its own — count how many stack up.

## The photos

- **Too few, too perfect.** Three or four studio-grade shots, no candid ones, no friends in frame, no ordinary days.
- **Faces the same way in every photo.** A stolen set usually comes from one person's best-of gallery.
- **No photo that could not be staged.** Ask for something specific and mundane — holding a spoon, a hand next to their face. A catfish cannot produce it.
- **Cropped edges and watermark remnants.** Somebody else's frame was cut away.
- **The face looks generated.** Check the ears, the earrings, the teeth, the background text and the hairline. AI portraits still fail there.

## The account

- Created weeks ago, with very few followers, or thousands of followers and almost no interaction.
- No tags from other people. Real accounts accumulate them.
- The bio's city, job and university do not appear anywhere else.
- The handle is a common name plus random digits.

## The conversation

- **They move you off the platform fast** — to WhatsApp or Telegram, where there is no moderation and no report button.
- **Strong feelings, very early.** Affection out of proportion to how long you have talked is a technique, not a personality.
- **Video is always impossible.** Broken camera, bad signal, the same excuse each time.
- **A crisis appears** — a medical bill, a customs fee, a stuck transfer — and it needs money, gift cards, or crypto.
- **An investment opportunity appears.** This is the most common romance-scam ending today.

Money moving in any direction is the point at which it stops being a doubt and becomes a scam.

## Checking the photo

Two searches take about two minutes:

1. **Reverse image search** (Google Images, TinEye) finds the exact file if it was scraped from a public page or a stock library.
2. **Reverse face search** finds the same *face* in different photos — a different pose, a different year, a different crop. That is what catches a stolen photo set, which is what an image search misses.

If the same face turns up under a different name on other platforms, with more history and more ordinary photos, you have found the real owner and your match is not them.

You can run this from the command line with the [examples in this repository](../README.md), or in a browser at [traceaifacescan.app](https://traceaifacescan.app/?utm_source=github&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=catfish-doc).

## If you find one

- **Do not confront them with the evidence.** They will vanish and reuse the photos on the next target.
- **Report the account** to the platform, with the link to the real profile.
- If money changed hands, report it where you live — [IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/) in the US, [Action Fraud](https://www.actionfraud.police.uk/) in the UK — and tell your bank immediately.
- If it was your own photos being used, ask the platform for removal; most have a dedicated impersonation form.

## What this is not for

Checking on a partner, tracking somebody who does not want to be found, or building a file on a person. Looking up a stranger's face to learn who they are is the exact harm these tools are regulated for, and it is forbidden by the terms of every service mentioned here. The legitimate question is narrow: *is this profile who it claims to be?*
